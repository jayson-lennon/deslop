# Anthropic Is Destroying Books for Claude

Target: 8–12 minutes. Headers and bracketed cues are for editing, not narration.

---

## Opening

So check this out. Anthropic has this thing called Project Panama, and the description of it is absolutely insane.

In an internal document that ended up in court, they ask, “What is Project Panama?” And the answer is, “Project Panama is our effort to destructively scan all the books in the world.” [SCREEN: Project Panama memo, highlight the quote]

Okay. So that does not sound very good.

Then the document asks, “Why use a codename?” And it says, “We use a ‘soft codename’ for it because we don’t want it to be known that we are working on this.” [SCREEN: highlight the second quote]

So Anthropic has a secret project to destroy all the books, and apparently they thought the name should be Panama. Which is kind of funny, because the Panama Papers are one of the most famous examples of rich people hiding things they did not want everyone else to see. Maybe that was not intentional. Maybe they just liked the name. But it is a pretty bad choice for a secret project that you do not want anybody to know about.

And what Project Panama actually was is this: Anthropic wanted a lot of books to train Claude. So they bought physical books, took the bindings off, scanned every page, kept the digital files, and then got rid of the physical books.

They bought the book, extracted the part they wanted, and threw the rest in the trash. Very normal behavior from the company that wants to make a thoughtful, safe AI assistant.

Now, before somebody shows up in the comments and says, “They bought the books, they can do whatever they want with them,” yes. That is generally how buying things works. If I buy a sandwich, I can eat it. I can also throw it away. I can probably scan it first if I have a very strange afternoon planned.

But the thing that interests me here is what this says about how these companies think about human culture. They look at a book and see a container full of valuable training data. They buy the container, remove the useful information, keep it as a private advantage for Claude, and destroy the object once it stops making money.

That is the whole AI business model in miniature.

## What does “destructive scanning” mean?

So when I first heard “destructive scanning,” I thought maybe this was just some dramatic headline about putting books on a scanner. But no, the scanning is destructive because you destroy the book in order to scan it faster.

The court describes Anthropic buying copyrighted books, tearing off the bindings, scanning every page, and storing the result in digitized, searchable files. [SCREEN: court opinion, highlight the description]

Basically, you remove the spine, separate the pages, and feed the whole thing through industrial equipment. It is much faster than opening a book and turning each page like a normal person who has not been given a warehouse and a budget.

The result is a digital copy that Anthropic can search and use for training. The paper book is now just a pile of pages that has completed its one job and can be recycled.

That is what makes the whole thing so bleak. A book can be read by thousands of people. It can sit in a library for decades. It can be passed to a friend, sold in a used bookstore, donated, found in an attic, or pulled off a shelf by somebody who has no idea it exists until that exact moment.

Anthropic looks at all of that and says, “No, the important part is the text. The physical thing is just inefficient storage.” Then it puts the book through the corporate woodchipper and keeps the words for Claude.

The end result is a private digital library built from human-created work, possibly containing copies of rare books that now have no chance of ever being read again. Anthropic gets to hoard the content inside the company, and the physical source copy disappears. Human beings spend centuries creating knowledge, then a company puts a price on the container, takes the valuable part, and throws it behind a corporate wall.

It is like if somebody bought every cookbook in a town, copied all the recipes into a private database, and then said, “Good news, we have improved cooking. Also, the books are gone now.”

## Why did Anthropic want physical books?

Now, the reason Anthropic wanted books is actually pretty easy to understand.

AI companies need huge amounts of text to train their models. The internet used to look like an unlimited supply of human writing, but now a lot of the internet is spam, duplicated content, SEO garbage, and articles that were generated by another AI that was trained on an earlier pile of garbage.

So the companies have created a machine that fills the internet with even more junk, and now they have to go back to the old human-written material because they need something good to train on. It's a destructive loop that attempts to capture anything and everything. First, the AI companies pollute the information supply with their models. Then they buy warehouses full of human-written books to escape the pollution they helped create. And finally they get to sell a product that you are forced to buy because they destroyed all the original content in the process.

So where does Anthropic get the books? A company called ISBNdb, which sells book-sourcing services to AI companies, says, “The world’s best AI training data is sitting on a shelf.” [SCREEN: 404 Media article]

Their marketing describes books as “curated, peer-reviewed, domain-specific human knowledge” and says that they are “dense, edited, authoritative.”

Books contain writing that people researched, edited, organized, and published. Somebody spent years learning a subject and turned that knowledge into a coherent book. People printed it, distributed it, bought it, and kept it available.

Then Anthropic shows up and says, “This is excellent. We need millions of copies.”

The company gets to take the work of authors, editors, publishers, but the resulting training resource belongs to Anthropic. It's no longer sitting in a public library and you can't just walk into a building and browse the secret Anthropic corpus. The information has been turned into fuel for a commercial product that Anthropic controls.

It is hard to look at that and see respect for literature. It a resource extraction industry that discovered books and decided the next step was to mine them.

## How many books are we talking about?

The scale is also ridiculous.

One vendor document discussed finding a scanning company capable of processing between 500,000 to 2 million books over six months. [SCREEN: vendor range, clearly label it as planned capacity]

This is a whole industrial operation with suppliers, warehouses, staff, scanning equipment, cost estimates, and a process for moving books through the system as fast as possible.

The Washington Post reported that Anthropic started ramping up Project Panama in early 2024. There are photographs of warehouses full of books connected to the reporting, with shelves and boxes going back farther than the camera can reasonably show. [SCREEN: warehouse image]

This warehouse photograph makes it impossible to estimate how many books are actually in there. It could be a million books, or maybe we get lucky and it's a million copies of a few terrible books for an MBA curriculum.

The point is that this was designed as an industrial pipeline. The normal relationship with a book is reading it, lending it, selling it, and actually using it. The Anthropic version is procurement, scanning, data extraction, and disposal.

The book goes in as culture and perspectives, and comes out as a stream of generic next word predictions.

## The piracy part

Now, Project Panama became public through the copyright lawsuit brought by authors Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson.

Anthropic also used pirated digital book collections, including material from shadow libraries. The authors sued over that, and Anthropic later agreed to a reported $1.5 billion settlement involving pirated books. [SCREEN: AP headline and settlement figure]

That's separate from the physical books Anthropic purchased and destructively scanned.

The court treated the two situations differently. With the purchased books, Anthropic had physical copies, converted them into digital copies, and kept the digital files private. With the pirated books, the company had acquired copies from unauthorized sources. Those are different facts and different legal questions.

The legal distinction matters for accuracy, but it does not magically make the larger situation look good. A court can decide that a particular use is allowed under the law. That is a statement about what a company may do. It is not a gold star saying that the company has behaved in a socially admirable way.

You can legally buy a book and destroy it. You can legally buy a warehouse full of books and destroy those too. That does not mean the only possible response is to clap politely while the books are fed into a machine.

## Why this feels so bad

The part that bothers me is the combination of public culture and private control.

Anthropic did not write these books. It did not research the subjects. It did not spend years developing the ideas or the writing styles inside them. It found a way to acquire the finished work in bulk, convert it into an advantage for Claude, and keep the resulting resource private.

And because the physical books were no longer useful to the company, they became disposable.

That's the corporate logic here. If something can be bought, it becomes a commodity. If it can be converted into an asset, somebody wants to own the asset. If destroying the original makes the process faster or cheaper, then the original becomes waste. The only thing that counts is whether the company gets a better product and a stronger position against its competitors.

Meanwhile, the rest of us are told that this is innovation. We are supposed to look at a machine destroying books and imagine progress because the words are going into Claude instead of a library.

The company is not preserving knowledge for the public. It is making a private copy of the useful part and using that copy to sell access to a chatbot. If you want the machine to give the information back to you, you can ask Claude nicely and hope the subscription is still affordable. Although this will be difficult since you won't even be aware that a book exists since it was destroyed.

Anthropic captures the value it wants, stores it inside the company, and makes Claude stronger with a resource that other people cannot simply browse. Other companies can buy other books, but Anthropic has already taken the useful information from these ones and turned it into part of its own business.

That is capitalism doing what capitalism does. Take something created by people, enclose it, turn it into property, and then call the enclosure efficiency.

## Ending

The sentence that keeps sticking with me is the one explaining the codename: “We don’t want it to be known that we are working on this.”

Maybe Anthropic wanted ordinary business secrecy or maybe it knew that a warehouse full of books waiting to be cut apart would look bad in public. Maybe the company just wanted to avoid answering questions while it built the pipeline.

It's easy to see why the project was not advertised as, “We are taking millions of human-written books, ripping them apart, and keeping the useful parts for Claude.” “Training data acquisition” sounds much cleaner.

So yes, Anthropic bought the books. Yes, the court ruled in its favor on the purchased-book copying. And yes, legally, you can do a lot of things to a book after you buy it.

The moral question is still sitting there in the warehouse, waiting to be shredded.

Anthropic took human culture, processed it like an industrial commodity, kept the profitable part private, and discarded the physical object when it stopped producing value. Then it called the whole thing a project, gave it a secret name, and hoped nobody would notice.

I am supposed to look at that and call it progress?

It looks like a bunch of very rich companies fighting over who gets to "win" the race of a sycophantic chatbot, while the rest of us supply the books, the writing, the art, and the ideas that make the machine useful in the first place.

Anthropic did not write the books. It just found a way to buy them, destroy them, and keep the part it wanted.
