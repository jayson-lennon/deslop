# Anthropic Is Destroying Books for Claude

Target: 8–12 minutes. Headers and bracketed cues are for editing, not narration.

---

## Opening

So check this out. Anthropic has this thing called Project Panama, and the description of it is genuinely one of the craziest things I've read in a while, which is saying something, because I spend a lot of time reading about what these companies are up to.

In an internal document that ended up in court, they ask, "What is Project Panama?" And the answer is, and I quote, "Project Panama is our effort to destructively scan all the books in the world." [SCREEN: Project Panama memo, highlight the quote]

Okay. So that does not sound very good.

Then the document asks, "Why use a codename?" And it says, "We use a 'soft codename' for it because we don't want it to be known that we are working on this." [SCREEN: highlight the second quote]

Which is a sentence you really have to sit with for a second, because it means that somewhere inside Anthropic, a person typed out the words "we don't want it to be known that we are destroying books," hit enter, and moved on with their day like that was a completely reasonable thing to write down.

So Anthropic has (or had) a secret project to ingest and destroy books, and apparently they thought the codename should be Panama. Which is kind of funny, because the Panama Papers are one of the most famous examples of rich people hiding things they didn't want everyone else to see. So either somebody in that company has a genuinely incredible sense of irony or they have a genuinely terrible sense of what things sound like when you say them out loud.

And what Project Panama actually was is this: Anthropic wanted a lot of books to train Claude, so they bought physical books by the warehouse, took the bindings off, scanned every page, kept the digital files, and then got rid of the actual books.

Very normal behavior from the company that wants to make a thoughtful, safe AI assistant.

Now, before you go running into the comments saying, "They bought the books, they can do whatever they want with them," yes, that is generally how buying things works, because if I buy a sandwich I can eat it, or throw it away, or scan it first if I've got a really strange afternoon planned, and nobody is arguing with you about the sandwich.

But the thing that interests me here is what this says about how these companies think about human culture, because they look at a book and see a container full of valuable training data, and the container is not the part they want.

## What does "destructive scanning" mean?

So when I first heard "destructive scanning," I assumed this was just some dramatic headline about putting books on a scanner.

Nope.

The scanning is destructive in every definition of the term: you destroy the book to scan it faster.

The court describes Anthropic buying copyrighted books, tearing off the bindings, scanning every page, and storing the result in digitized, searchable files. [SCREEN: court opinion, highlight the description]

They remove the spine, separate the pages, and feed the whole thing through industrial equipment.

The result is a digital copy that Anthropic can search and use for training, and the paper book is now a pile of loose pages that has completed its one job and can be recycled.

That is the part that gets me, because a book can be read by many people, it can sit on a library shelf for decades, it can be passed to a friend, sold in a used bookstore, donated, found in an attic, or pulled off a shelf by somebody who had no idea it existed until that exact moment, and every one of those futures is now gone. Anthropic looks at all of that and thought, "No, the important part is the text. The physical thing is just inefficient storage." Then it shoves the book through the corporate woodchipper and keeps the words for Claude.

That is how you get from "human beings spent centuries creating knowledge" to "a company put a price on the container, took the valuable part, and threw it behind a corporate paywall."

## Why did Anthropic want physical books?

Now, the reason Anthropic wanted books is actually pretty easy to understand.

AI companies need huge amounts of text to train their models, and the internet, which used to look like an unlimited supply of human writing, is now spam, duplicated pages, SEO garbage, and articles generated by another AI that was itself trained on an earlier pile of garbage, which means the good stuff is getting harder to find exactly because of what these companies have been doing.

So they've created a machine that fills the internet with even more junk, and now they have to go back to the old human-written material because they need something clean to train on. It's a destructive loop that attempts to capture anything and everything. First, the AI companies pollute the information supply with their models. Then they buy warehouses full of human-written books to escape the pollution they helped create. And finally they get to sell a product that you're forced to buy because they destroyed all the original content in the process.

Beautiful system. Fantastic.

So where does Anthropic get the books? A company called ISBNdb, which sells book-sourcing services to AI companies, says, "The world's best AI training data is sitting on a shelf." [SCREEN: 404 Media article]

Their marketing describes books as "curated, peer-reviewed, domain-specific human knowledge" and says that they are "dense, edited, authoritative."

Which is true, and that's the funny part, because somebody spent years learning a subject and turning that knowledge into a coherent book, other people edited it and printed it and distributed it and bought it and kept it available, and all of that is human effort.

Then Anthropic shows up and says, "This is excellent. We need millions of copies."

The company gets to take the work of authors, editors, and publishers, but the resulting training resource belongs to Anthropic, so it's no longer sitting in a public library and you can't just walk into Anthropic's HQ and browse their secret corpus.

It's just standard capitalist resource extraction. Anthropic discovered books and decided the next step was to mine them.

## How many books are we talking about?

The scale is also ridiculous.

One vendor document discussed finding a scanning company capable of processing between 500,000 to 2 million books over six months. [SCREEN: vendor range, clearly label it as planned capacity]

Half a million. As the starting number. (Do the math for daily)

That is a whole industrial operation with suppliers, warehouses, staff, scanning equipment, cost estimates, and a process for moving books through the system as fast as possible, which is a very different thing from a guy with a flatbed scanner and a dream.

The Washington Post reported that Anthropic started ramping up Project Panama in early 2024, and there is at least one photograph of a warehouses full of books connected to the reporting, with shelves and boxes going back farther than the camera can reasonably show. [SCREEN: warehouse image]

Look at this thing. It just keeps going.

There is no way to estimate how many books are actually in that photograph. It could be a million books, or it could be a million copies of the same terrible book that MBAs keep buying for each other.

The point is that this was designed as an industrial pipeline. Books go in one end, and a stream of generic next word predictions comes out the other.

## The piracy part

Oh, and one more thing. This is the same company that also got busted for pirating books.

Project Panama only became public because of a lawsuit from authors Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson, and along with buying books, Anthropic had been hoovering up pirated digital collections from shadow libraries, which is the part of the story that ended with a reported $1.5 billion settlement. [SCREEN: AP headline and settlement figure]

So the timeline here is great. First they just downloaded the books for free like everybody's sketchiest nephew. Then, once that became a problem, they scaled up to buying books by the warehouse so they could destroy them faster. Same goal, more forklifts.

The pirated copies and the purchased books are different situations, and I'm not going to spend ten minutes doing a copyright-law lecture nobody asked for. The part I actually care about is simpler: this is a company that wanted all the books, and it kept finding new ways to get them until it ended up literally shredding them.

## Why this feels so bad

The part that bothers me is the combination of public culture and private control.

Anthropic did not write these books. Did not research the subjects. Did not spend years developing the ideas or the writing styles inside them. It found a way to acquire the finished work in bulk, convert it into an advantage for Claude, and keep the resulting resource private.

And because the physical books were no longer useful to the company, they became disposable. That is the corporate logic: extract the value, treat the remains as waste. Meanwhile, the rest of us are told this is innovation. Look at the machine destroying books. Isn't progress wonderful? The words are going into Claude instead of a library.

The company is not preserving knowledge for the public, and the public is not getting a library out of this. It was destroyed. That was the whole point.

Anthropic captures the value it wants, stores it inside the company, and makes Claude stronger with a resource that other people cannot simply browse.

That is capitalism doing what capitalism does. Take something created by people, enclose it, turn it into property, and then call the enclosure efficiency.

## Ending

The sentence that keeps sticking with me is the one explaining the codename: "We don't want it to be known that we are working on this."

Maybe Anthropic wanted ordinary business secrecy, or maybe it knew that a warehouse full of books waiting to be cut apart would look bad in public, or maybe the company wanted to avoid answering questions while it built the pipeline, and honestly it doesn't matter which one it is: any of them lands in the same place.

It's easy to see why the project was not advertised as, "We are taking millions of human-written books, ripping them apart, and keeping the useful parts for Claude." "Training data acquisition" sounds much cleaner.

So yes, Anthropic bought the books. Yes, the court ruled in its favor on the purchased-book copying. And yes, legally, you can do a lot of things to a book after you buy it.

The moral question is still sitting there in the warehouse, waiting to be shredded.

Anthropic took human culture, processed it like an industrial commodity, kept the profitable part private, and discarded the physical object when it stopped producing value, and then it called the whole thing a project, gave it a secret name, and hoped nobody would notice.

The only reason any of this came out is that three authors had the standing to sue.

All of it looks like a bunch of very rich companies fighting over who gets to "win" the race of building a sycophantic chatbot, while the rest of us supply the books, the writing, the art, and the ideas that make the machine useful in the first place.

Anthropic did not write the books. It just found a way to buy them, destroy them, and keep the part it wanted.
